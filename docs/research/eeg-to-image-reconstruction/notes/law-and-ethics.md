# Law, regulation and ethics of EEG / neural data and brain-decoding systems (status as of 1 October 2026)

This is informational research, not legal advice. Statuses were checked against sources dated up to September 2026. Where sources disagree, both are given. The system in scope records EEG and physiology, stores encrypted session datasets locally, trains personalized models, may send derived text prompts to cloud image generators, and writes keyed fingerprints (no raw data) to a provenance hash chain. The developer is in Oklahoma and may distribute the software publicly.

## 1. US neural-data laws: state statutes, Oklahoma, federal bills, FTC and FDA positions

### Takeaway
By October 2026 four US states have enforceable neural-data rules: Colorado (in force Aug 2024), California (Jan 2025), Montana (Oct 2025) and Connecticut (1 July 2026). Vermont enacted a rights declaration plus a study (May 2026) with no enforcement. Oklahoma's new comprehensive privacy law (signed 20 March 2026, in force 1 January 2027) does not mention neural data. The federal MIND Act (S.2925) is still in committee. Neither the FTC nor the FDA has issued a neural-data-specific rule or enforcement action. The definitions differ on three points: central vs peripheral nervous system, whether the data must be used for identification, and whether inferences are excluded. Whether text decoded from EEG counts as "neural data" is unsettled.

### Cited Findings

**Colorado: HB 24-1058 (amends the Colorado Privacy Act, CPA)**
- Signed 17 April 2024; effective 6/7 August 2024. Colorado was the first state to extend a comprehensive privacy law to neural data explicitly. [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/colorado-amends-privacy-act-with-h-b-1058-adding-new-protections-for-biological-and-neural-data); signed text: [Colorado legislature](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_1058_signed.pdf). (Hunton says "August 6" and FPF says "Aug 7, 2024". The difference is minor and unresolved.)
- Definition: "information that is generated by the measurement of the activity of an individual's central or peripheral nervous systems and that can be processed by or with the assistance of a device." Neural data sits inside a new "biological data" category, which is sensitive data only when "used or intended to be used, singly or in combination with other personal data, for identification purposes." [FPF, 12 Aug 2025](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/); [InsideBCI, Apr 2026](https://insidebci.com/policy/2026-04-03-us-states-build-patchwork-of-neural-data-privacy-laws-as-bci-market-accelerates/)
- Obligations: opt-in consent before processing sensitive data, and data protection assessments that document benefits, risks and mitigations. [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/colorado-amends-privacy-act-with-h-b-1058-adding-new-protections-for-biological-and-neural-data); [Arnold & Porter, Jul 2025](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2025/07/neural-data-privacy-regulation)
- Consumer rights under the CPA: opt out of targeted advertising, sale and significant-decision profiling, plus the general CPA rights (access, correction, deletion, portability). [Privacy Rights Clearinghouse / CPA overviews](https://privacyrights.org/resources-tools/law-overviews/colorado-privacy-act)
- Applicability: controllers processing data of at least 100,000 Colorado consumers per year, or at least 25,000 if they earn anything from selling personal data. There is no revenue threshold. Employees and B2B contacts are excluded from "consumer." [Enzuzo CPA guide](https://www.enzuzo.com/blog/the-colorado-privacy-act); [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2025/07/neural-data-privacy-regulation)

**California: SB 1223 (amends the CCPA)**
- Chaptered 28 September 2024 (Stats. 2024, ch. 887); effective 1 January 2025. [California Legislative Information](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240SB1223). (One Vermont committee briefing note, dated 9 April 2026, wrongly gives "2023 / Jan 1 2024": [Vermont legislature PDF](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Workgroups/Senate%20Health%20and%20Welfare/Bills/H.814/Witness%20Testimony/H.814~Ashley%20Collins~US%20State%20Legislation%20to%20Protect%20Neural%20Data~4-9-2026.pdf). The chaptered record is authoritative.)
- Definition: "information that is generated by measuring the activity of a consumer's central or peripheral nervous system, and that is not inferred from nonneural information." Neural data is added to "sensitive personal information" at Cal. Civ. Code §1798.140(ae)(1)(G). [leginfo](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240SB1223)
- Consent model: no opt-in. Consumers get the CCPA "right to limit" use of sensitive personal information to what is necessary to provide the goods or services an average consumer would expect. This is an opt-out for uses beyond the requested service. The CCPA also covers employees and B2B contexts. [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2025/07/neural-data-privacy-regulation); [leginfo digest](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240SB1223)
- CCPA applicability includes a revenue threshold of roughly $25M (inflation-adjusted). [Enzuzo comparison](https://www.enzuzo.com/blog/the-colorado-privacy-act)
- Follow-on bill SB 44 (Umberg, 2025) would have limited BCI providers to using neural data "only for the purpose for which the neural data was collected" and required deletion once that purpose was met. It was held in Senate Appropriations on 23 May 2025 and returned to the Secretary of Senate on 2 February 2026 under Joint Rule 56. **It failed and is not law.** [leginfo SB 44 status](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billStatusClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB44); [CalMatters Digital Democracy](https://calmatters.digitaldemocracy.org/bills/ca_202520260sb44)

**Montana: SB 163 (amends the Genetic Information Privacy Act)**
- Became law in early May 2025 and took effect 1 October 2025. The sources disagree on how it became law. KFF Health News reports that Governor Gianforte signed it on 1 May 2025: [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/mental-health/colorado-california-montana-states-neural-data-privacy-laws-neurorights/). A secondary employment-law source says it became law without his signature on 2 May 2025: [Vensure](https://vensure.com/employment-law-updates/montana-sets-new-standard-for-brain-data-privacy-with-neurotechnology-law/). This is unresolved. Montana's Chapter 345 print is here: [archive.legmt.gov](https://archive.legmt.gov/content/Sessions/69th/Contractor_index/CH0345.pdf).
- Covers "neurotechnology data": information captured by neurotechnologies, generated by measuring neural activity, or associated with neural activity. It excludes "downstream physical effects" such as pupil dilation and breathing rate, and excludes nonneural inferences. [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/)
- The sources disagree on scope. Cooley says the law reaches all "entities that handle neural data of Montana residents": [Cooley, Apr 2025](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-04-22-montana-on-the-brain-a-bold-step-for-neural-privacy). FPF says it applies only to entities that offer genetic testing or collect genetic data: [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/). The statute text should be checked.
- Obligations are the most detailed of any state:
  - express consent for collection and use;
  - **separate** express consent, naming the recipient, for any transfer to a third party other than processors;
  - separate consent for use beyond the primary purpose;
  - informed express consent for transfers for research;
  - consent for marketing and for sale;
  - a process to access and delete data and to revoke consent;
  - a "comprehensive security program";
  - a valid legal process (warrant or subpoena) before disclosure to law enforcement.
  [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-04-22-montana-on-the-brain-a-bold-step-for-neural-privacy); [Vensure](https://vensure.com/employment-law-updates/montana-sets-new-standard-for-brain-data-privacy-with-neurotechnology-law/); [Risk Management Magazine, Feb 2026](https://www.rmmagazine.com/articles/article/2026/02/24/state-of-mind--the-new-landscape-of-neural-data-privacy-laws)

**Connecticut: SB 1295 (amends the CTDPA)**
- Signed 24 June 2025; effective 1 July 2026. [Risk Management Magazine](https://www.rmmagazine.com/articles/article/2026/02/24/state-of-mind--the-new-landscape-of-neural-data-privacy-laws)
- Definition: "any information generated by measuring the activity of an individual's central nervous system." It covers the central nervous system only, so peripheral and physiological signals are not neural data. It has no carve-out for inferred data. [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/)
- Neural data is "sensitive data." Processing is barred unless reasonably necessary or the consumer expressly consents, and sensitive data cannot be sold without consent. [Risk Management Magazine](https://www.rmmagazine.com/articles/article/2026/02/24/state-of-mind--the-new-landscape-of-neural-data-privacy-laws); [Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/connecticut-data-privacy-act-2026-amendments/)
- **Applicability changed on 1 July 2026.** The threshold fell from 100,000 to 35,000 consumers, and any controller that processes **any volume of sensitive data** or sells personal data is now covered. Privacy notices must say whether personal data is used to train large language models. The 60-day cure period was removed. [Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/connecticut-data-privacy-act-2026-amendments/); [Ice Miller](https://www.icemiller.com/thought-leadership/state-privacy-law-updates-july-2026-connecticut-arkansas-utah)

**Oklahoma**
- SB 546 (Oklahoma Data Privacy Act) was signed 20 March 2026 and takes effect **1 January 2027**. One vendor blog says July 2026; law-firm sources say 1 January 2027. [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20260327-oklahoma-enacts-nations-twentieth-state-comprehensive-privacy-law); [McAfee & Taft](https://www.mcafeetaft.com/signed-sealed-and-soon-to-be-delivered-into-law-oklahomas-new-data-privacy-law-becomes-effective-january-1-2027/)

### Inferences
- **Who is a "controller."** If the software runs entirely on the user's PC and the developer never receives the data, the developer probably does not "process" consumers' personal data. The state privacy statutes then bind the developer weakly or not at all. The person running it on themselves is not a business. This changes the moment the developer offers any hosted service (sync, cloud training, telemetry, shared answers) that receives session data or derived prompts. The cloud image provider becomes a recipient or third party.
- **Connecticut is the strictest trigger for a small developer.** Since 1 July 2026 it has no volume threshold for sensitive data. Any hosted feature that receives EEG-derived data from a Connecticut resident would probably bring the operator into scope, with consent required.
- **Is a decoded text prompt "neural data"?** Under California the answer is unsettled. The exclusion covers data "inferred from **nonneural** information," and a prompt decoded from EEG is inferred *from* neural information, which arguably keeps it inside the definition. Under Connecticut, which has no inference carve-out, it is plausibly covered. Under Colorado it is covered only if used for identification. Treat derived prompts as neural data for design purposes.
- **Physiology channels.** ECG, EDA, respiration and PPG are peripheral or autonomic signals. California, Colorado and the MIND Act (peripheral nervous system included) may cover them. Connecticut (central nervous system only) does not. Montana excludes "downstream physical effects" such as breathing rate.
- **Montana's named-recipient consent.** If Montana's broad-scope reading is right, sending a derived prompt to a named cloud generator needs separate express consent that names the recipient. The project's existing "remote generators need https and a yes" gate is close to this, but the consent text should name the provider.

## 2. International: Chile, EU GDPR, EU AI Act, UNESCO, OECD

### Takeaway
Chile constitutionally protects "brain activity and the information derived from it" (Law 21.383, 25 Oct 2021). In Girardi v. Emotiv (Rol 105065-2023, 9 Aug 2023) its Supreme Court ordered Emotiv to delete a user's brain data.

In the EU, data protection authorities treat most neurodata as special-category data under GDPR Article 9, as biometric or health data, and as almost never truly anonymous. The AI Act bans emotion inference from biometric data only in workplaces and schools (since 2 February 2025). Other emotion-recognition systems are Annex III high-risk, but the Digital Omnibus pushed that deadline to 2 December 2027. Article 50 transparency duties have applied since 2 August 2026.

UNESCO (November 2025) and the OECD (December 2019) instruments are non-binding.

### Cited Findings

**Chile**
- Law No. 21.383 (25 October 2021) amended Article 19 No. 1 of the Constitution to protect physical and psychic integrity, with scientific and technological development required to respect them. It specifically safeguards "brain activity" and "the information derived from it." [Stanford Law and Biosciences Blog, 27 Apr 2026](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/04/27/even-chiles-neurorights-leave-inferred-mental-data-in-a-gray-zone/)
- **Girardi/Emotiv Inc., Rol No. 105065-2023, decided 9 August 2023.** Former senator Guido Girardi used Emotiv's Insight headset. The Supreme Court held that Emotiv's storage and commercial use of his brain-activity data violated his physical and psychic integrity and privacy rights (Constitution Arts. 1 and 4/19). It ordered deletion of all stored data linked to his use of the device. [Stanford Law blog](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/04/27/even-chiles-neurorights-leave-inferred-mental-data-in-a-gray-zone/); [Cornejo-Plaza et al., Frontiers/PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10929545/)
- The Neurorights Foundation described it as the first court ruling in the world against a neurotech company on neurorights. [Neurorights Foundation (X)](https://twitter.com/neuro_rights/status/1691118464872689668)
- In 2024 Chile's Public Health Institute (ISP) classified the Emotiv Insight as non-medical, because it targets "research applications and personal use." The Stanford commentary argues that information *inferred* from neural signals by consumer software remains a regulatory "gray zone" even in Chile. [Stanford Law blog](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/04/27/even-chiles-neurorights-leave-inferred-mental-data-in-a-gray-zone/)

**EU GDPR (Regulation 2016/679)** ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj))
- The EDPS and Spain's AEPD issued the joint TechDispatch #1/2024 on Neurodata (3 June 2024). It says neurodata "often constitute special categories of personal data," either biometric data that can uniquely identify a person or data concerning health. Even if a person is not identified at collection, they "would remain identifiable" because neurodata allow unique identification. [EDPS TechDispatch page](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2024-06-03-techdispatch-12024-neurodata_en); [Morrison Foerster summary, Jul 2024](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240722-it-s-all-in-your-head-not-anymore-eu-data-protection)
- The TechDispatch also:
  - stresses proportionality and data minimization, because continuous brain recording produces large, intrusive datasets;
  - warns that accuracy is limited by brain plasticity and collection error;
  - flags inference of intimate thoughts "that do not translate into actions";
  - urges fairness assessments for AI-driven processing;
  - says "brain fingerprinting" should be limited to healthcare.
  [MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240722-it-s-all-in-your-head-not-anymore-eu-data-protection)
- The Global Privacy Assembly adopted a resolution on neurotechnologies in November 2024. [EDPS-hosted GPA resolution PDF](https://www.edps.europa.eu/system/files/2024-11/resolution-on-neurotechnologies_en.pdf). The Berlin Group (International Working Group on Data Protection in Technology) published a working paper on neurotechnologies on 15 May 2025. [BfDI PDF](https://www.bfdi.bund.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/EN/Berlin-Group/20250515-WP-Neurotechnologies.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=2). I could not parse the contents of either; see Gaps.
- Relevant GDPR mechanics, from the regulation text on EUR-Lex:
  - Art. 4(14) defines biometric data as data from specific technical processing of physical, physiological or behavioural characteristics that allow or confirm unique identification.
  - Art. 9(1) prohibits processing special categories unless an Art. 9(2) exception applies, such as explicit consent (9(2)(a)) or scientific research with safeguards (9(2)(j) with Art. 89).
  - Art. 35 requires a DPIA for large-scale special-category processing.
  - Art. 25 requires data protection by design and default.
  - Art. 2(2)(c) exempts purely personal or household activity.
  - Art. 3(2) applies the GDPR to non-EU controllers who offer goods or services to, or monitor, people in the EU.
  [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **Hashes on a chain.** The EDPB's Guidelines 02/2025 on blockchain were adopted as v1.1 on 8 April 2025, and the final v2.0 on 7 July 2026. They say a salted or keyed hash stored on-chain "will itself constitute personal data." They recommend keeping the original data off-chain and using cryptographic commitments, so that deleting the original and its witness leaves the on-chain value useless. They say technical impossibility does not excuse failing to honour erasure. [EDPB guidelines PDF (v1.1)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2025-04/edpb_guidelines_202502_blockchain_en.pdf); [Bird & Bird on final v2.0, 2026](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/netherlands/edpb-adopts-final-guidelines-on-blockchain-and-personal-data-a-practical-guide-for-organisations)

**EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689)** ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj))
- Art. 3(39) defines an emotion recognition system as "an AI system for the purpose of identifying or inferring emotions or intentions of natural persons on the basis of their biometric data." [William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/the-time-to-ai-act-is-now-a-practical-guide-to-emotion-recognition-systems-under-the-ai-act/)
- Art. 5(1)(f) prohibits AI that infers emotions in **workplaces and education institutions**, except for medical or safety reasons. It has applied since 2 February 2025. Recital 44 cites the limited reliability, specificity and generalisability of such systems. [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/); [William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/the-time-to-ai-act-is-now-a-practical-guide-to-emotion-recognition-systems-under-the-ai-act/)

  - political agreement 7 May 2026; Parliament endorsement 16 June 2026; Council adoption 29 June 2026;
  - published in the Official Journal 24 July 2026; in force 27 July 2026;
  - Annex III high-risk obligations deferred to **2 December 2027**, and Annex I to 2 August 2028;
  - **Article 50 transparency duties are unchanged and have applied since 2 August 2026**. Under these, deployers of emotion-recognition systems must inform the people exposed;
  - two new Art. 5 prohibitions phase in on 2 December 2026.
  [Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/eu-ai-act-high-risk-delay-article-50-transparency-consent/); [Jones Walker](https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/ai-law-blog/yes-august-2-still-matters-the-eu-approved-a-high-risk-ai-delay-but-most-trans.html?id=102nbon); [DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/2026/The-Digital-AI-Omnibus-Proposed-deferral-of-high-risk-AI-obligations-under-the-AI-Act)
- The Commission's guidelines on prohibited practices say Art. 5(1)(f) targets systems that actually "infer emotions," not anything merely labelled "emotion recognition." [William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/the-time-to-ai-act-is-now-a-practical-guide-to-emotion-recognition-systems-under-the-ai-act/)

**UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Neurotechnology**
- Adopted by the 43rd General Conference in Samarkand, Uzbekistan, in November 2025. Global Policy Watch gives 12 November 2025. The document reference on the Johns Hopkins copy is dated 11.11.2025. [UNESCO legal affairs page](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-ethics-neurotechnology); [Global Policy Watch, Jan 2026](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/01/unesco-adopts-first-global-framework-on-neurotechnology-ethics/); [text, JHU Berman Institute copy](https://bioethics.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/UNESCO-RECOMMENDATION-on-the-Ethics-of-Neurotechnology-11.11.2025.pdf)
- It is non-binding but may be persuasive authority. It covers the whole life cycle from design to disposal. Its content includes:
  - freely given, informed consent and the right to choose whether to use neurotechnology "at any time";
  - data minimization;
  - strong data protection and cybersecurity;
  - no use of neural data in recommender systems for manipulative purposes, and limits on nudging and neuromarketing;
  - for children, use limited to medical, therapeutic or "well-justified" purposes, with advice against non-therapeutic use.
  [Global Policy Watch](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/01/unesco-adopts-first-global-framework-on-neurotechnology-ethics/)

**OECD Recommendation on Responsible Innovation in Neurotechnology (OECD/LEGAL/0457, 2019)**
- The first international standard in the field. It has nine principles; **Principle 7 is "safeguard personal brain data and other information."** It defines personal brain data as "data relating to the functioning or structure of the human brain of an identified or identifiable individual that includes unique information about their physiology, health or mental states." [OECD Neurotech Toolkit PDF](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/topics/policy-sub-issues/emerging-technologies/neurotech-toolkit.pdf); [OECD archived recommendation text](https://web-archive-storage.oecd.org/aemint-web-archive-prod/web-archive/c5/c5a97124d0b9d04ca1919f6f60cca16b05a0ece855f16718ab154194594b39cb.pdf); [EDPS TechDispatch quoting OECD](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2024-06-03-techdispatch-12024-neurodata_en)
- It is a non-binding Council recommendation. The OECD legal-instruments page ([OECD-LEGAL-0457](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/en/instruments/OECD-LEGAL-0457)) did not render, so the exact adoption day was not verified here.

### Inferences
- **EU users of a public release.** If the release reaches EU users and the developer runs any hosted component, the developer is plausibly a GDPR controller under Art. 3(2). It would then need explicit consent under Art. 9(2)(a), a DPIA and privacy by design. Fully local use by an individual on themselves falls under the household exemption for that user. The developer's position then depends on whether it receives any data.
- **The provenance chain.** Under the EDPB blockchain guidelines, keyed digests of personal session datasets on a public, permanent ledger are still personal data if the key holder can link them. The project's "keyed digests only" design matches the recommended commitment pattern, but erasure must be achievable by destroying the key and the off-chain data. That property should be documented.
- **AI Act classification of EEG-to-image.** EEG is a physiological characteristic, so it plausibly counts as "biometric data" under the AI Act's Art. 3(34) concept. An EEG-to-image system that decodes *perceived or imagined visual content* is not by definition an emotion-recognition system. If it infers affect or valence (for example to condition images on mood), it would fall into the Art. 3(39) definition and therefore Art. 50 transparency now and Annex III high-risk from December 2027 if placed on the EU market. Use in workplaces or schools would be prohibited. The AI Act's exemptions for pure scientific R&D (Art. 2(6)/(8)) and purely personal, non-professional use by individuals may apply to a research prototype. This classification is unsettled.

### Gaps
- I could not access the full Chilean ruling, the Frontiers/PMC article (blocked by reCAPTCHA) or details of the ISP/customs orders. I did not confirm whether Chile's new data protection law (Law 21.719, 2024) defines neural data.
- The contents of the Berlin Group working paper (May 2025) and the GPA resolution (2024) could not be parsed (binary PDFs).
- No Court of Justice of the EU ruling or national DPA enforcement specific to EEG was found.
- Brazil, Mexico, Spain's Digital Rights Charter and Council of Europe work on neurotech were not researched.
- Whether EEG counts as "biometric data" under the AI Act when it is not used for identification has not been authoritatively settled.

## 3. Mental privacy: inferring thoughts, memories and emotions, secondary use, and sending derived data to third-party AI

### Takeaway
The literature and regulators agree that neural data can reveal intimate mental states, including ones never expressed, and that it identifies individuals. The main live debate is that the laws key on the *source* (neural measurement), while the harm comes from the *inference*. Derived outputs, such as text decoded from brain signals and sent to a third-party model, sit in a regulatory gray zone.

Consent for secondary use is weakly protected in practice. A 2024 audit of 30 consumer neurotech companies found that 29 had unrestricted access to neural data and 96.7% reserved the right to share it. One mitigating fact is that state-of-the-art semantic decoding has so far needed the subject's cooperation both to train and to apply.

### Cited Findings
- EDPS/AEPD: neural data may "reveal the most intimate personal thoughts and feelings, including those that do not translate into actions and consequently cannot be measured or inferred by data collected through other technologies." [EDPS TechDispatch](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2024-06-03-techdispatch-12024-neurodata_en)
- Tang, LeBel, Jain and Huth, *Nature Neuroscience* (May 2023), reconstructed continuous language from non-invasive fMRI, including imagined speech and silent video. They found subject cooperation was required both to train and to apply the decoder, which they framed as a mental-privacy safeguard. [Nature Neuroscience](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-023-01304-9); [ScienceDaily/UT Austin](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/05/230501114006.htm)
- The Neurorights Foundation report "Safeguarding Brain Data: Assessing the Privacy Practices of Consumer Neurotechnology Companies" (1 April 2024, 30 companies) found:
  - 29 of 30 companies had access to neural data with no limits;
  - 96.7% reserved the right to transfer brain data to third parties;
  - fewer than 20% mentioned encryption;
  - 16.7% committed to breach notification;
  - 10% adopted all core safeguards;
  - more than 60% did not disclose how neural data is managed.
  [TrustArc summary](https://trustarc.com/resource/neurotechnology-privacy-safeguarding-the-next-frontier-of-data/); [Neurorights Foundation reports page](https://www.neurorightsfoundation.org/research/reports)
- The "Goldilocks problem": the definitions are either too broad (covering all bodily data) or too narrow, and carve-outs for inferred or nonneural data may leave functionally equivalent mental inferences unprotected. [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/)
- "Beyond neural data: Cognitive biometrics and mental privacy," *Neuron* (2024). As the title indicates, it argues that mental privacy should cover "cognitive biometrics," meaning mental-state inferences from neural *and* non-neural signals, not only raw neural data. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0896627324006524) (The page returned 403, so only the title and venue were seen. The authors are commonly cited as Magee, Ienca and Farahany, but that was not verified here.)
- Horiuchi (arXiv 2608.00936, 2 August 2026) argues that "a source-bound rule therefore permits circumvention." He proposes a source-neutral trigger for "person-linked mental-state attribution," with tiered duties: elicitation, attribution and use are regulated cumulatively, and covert or coercive inference and non-consensual closed-loop intervention are presumptively prohibited. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00936)
- The Stanford Law commentary (April 2026) says that even under Chile's constitution, "information inferred from" neural signals by non-medical consumer software is ungoverned. [Stanford Law blog](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/04/27/even-chiles-neurorights-leave-inferred-mental-data-in-a-gray-zone/)
- UNESCO 2025 singles out manipulation through recommender systems and neuromarketing as harms. [Global Policy Watch](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/01/unesco-adopts-first-global-framework-on-neurotechnology-ethics/)
- The Senators' 2025 letter asked the FTC to require disclosure and transparency even for de-identified or anonymized neural data, and to study data transfers. That reflects concern about onward transfer. [Senate Commerce](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/press/dem/release/cantwell-schumer-markey-call-on-ftc-to-protect-consumers-neural-data-2025-4/)
- Montana requires separate express consent naming the third party for any non-processor transfer, and separate consent for use beyond the primary purpose. This is the clearest statutory rule on secondary use and onward transfer. [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-04-22-montana-on-the-brain-a-bold-step-for-neural-privacy)
- Connecticut now requires privacy notices to say whether personal data is used to train LLMs. That matters if a cloud provider may train on prompts. [Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/connecticut-data-privacy-act-2026-amendments/)

### Inferences
- **Why a decoded prompt is sensitive.** A text prompt decoded from EEG ("a red house by the sea") is a lossy, human-readable *attribution of mental content*. It is arguably more revealing to a third party than raw EEG, because it needs no decoder to interpret. Under source-neutral reasoning (Horiuchi; Magee et al.) and plausibly under California, Connecticut and Montana, it should be treated as neural or sensitive data.
- **Risks of sending it to a cloud generator:**
  - provider logging and retention;
  - provider training on prompts;
  - linkage to the user's account or IP address;
  - accumulation over sessions into a mental-content profile;
  - the prompt being wrong (decoding error) yet recorded as a fact about the person.
- **Mitigations:**
  - per-request opt-in that names the provider;
  - a preview of the exact prompt and a chance to edit it before sending;
  - stripping identifiers;
  - choosing provider settings or contracts with no training and no retention;
  - keeping a local log of what was sent;
  - a default of local generation.
- **Cooperation is a technical fact, not a legal one.** The finding that decoding needs subject cooperation supports a consent-centric design today. It is not a legal safeguard and may change as cross-subject models improve.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study specifically measuring privacy leakage from EEG-*derived text prompts* sent to commercial image-generation APIs.
- No regulator guidance addresses brain-to-prompt pipelines directly.
- The full texts of the Neuron paper and the Neurorights report were not read; their figures come from summaries.

## 4. Medical-device risk: when an EEG-to-image system is an FDA device, and what keeps it outside

### Takeaway
FDA jurisdiction turns on **intended use**, shown by labeling, claims and marketing, not on the technology. A clinical electroencephalograph is a Class II device (21 CFR 882.1400). Consumer EEG headbands with wellness claims (relaxation, meditation, focus, sleep) are routinely sold without FDA clearance under the general-wellness policy, which was revised on 6 January 2026.

An EEG-to-image tool stays outside device regulation if it is presented as research, entertainment or art and makes no claim to diagnose, treat, monitor or mitigate a disease or condition. Claims of assistive communication for patients, mental-health assessment or clinical-grade accuracy would pull it in.

### Cited Findings
- 21 CFR 882.1400 classifies an electroencephalograph ("a device used to measure and record the electrical activity of the patient's brain obtained by placing two or more electrodes on the head") as Class II (performance standards). [21 CFR 882.1400 via FindLaw](https://codes.findlaw.com/cfr/title-21-food-and-drugs/cfr-sect-21-882-1400/). Example 510(k) clearances under this regulation: [FDA K203827](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf20/K203827.pdf), [FDA K240993](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf24/K240993.pdf).
- Consumer neurofeedback headbands (for example Muse) are treated as low-risk general-wellness products under FDA enforcement discretion. Interaxon states that Muse "is not a medical device" and has no FDA clearance; it carries FCC, UL and CE marks. [IEEE Pulse](https://www.embs.org/pulse/articles/why-consumer-neurofeedback-devices-are-more-than-hype-for-brain-health/); [Muse legal page](https://choosemuse.com/pages/legal#privacy); [Cybernews review 2026](https://cybernews.com/health-tech/muse-s-review/)
- **FDA General Wellness guidance (6 January 2026).**
  - Products intended solely for general wellness and low risk are not actively regulated.
  - Software functions for a healthy lifestyle that are unrelated to disease are excluded by statute (§520(o)(1)(B)).
  - The guidance disqualifies:
    - disease or condition references and diagnostic thresholds;
    - claims of clinical equivalence or substitution;
    - treatment guidance or disease alerts;
    - unvalidated values that mimic clinical ones;
    - measuring physiology "for medical purposes such as screening, diagnosis, monitoring, alerting or management";
    - invasiveness.
  - The guidance does not mention EEG or neuro products specifically.
  [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products); [Arent Fox Schiff](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/fda-issues-updated-guidance-low-risk-general-wellness-devices-and-clinical); [CRS Insight IN12657](https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12657/IN12657.1.pdf)
- A neurotech regulatory newsletter reports that Zeto's EEG system was added to FDA's sensor-based digital health technology list in 2026. This concerns a clinical system and is not relevant to wellness classification. [search summary of Naveen Rao's Substack](https://neurotechnology.substack.com/p/reg6) (low confidence; not fetched)
- Chile's ISP reached the same kind of result for Emotiv Insight in 2024: non-medical, because it is aimed at "research applications and personal use, not diagnosis or treatment." [Stanford Law blog](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/04/27/even-chiles-neurorights-leave-inferred-mental-data-in-a-gray-zone/)
- HIPAA generally does not reach direct-to-consumer neurotech makers that are not covered entities. [DWT](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/05/all-the-neural-data-and-no-common-rule-2026)

### Inferences
- **Claims that would likely make an EEG-to-image system a device** (based on the device definition and the 2026 guidance criteria above):
  - a communication aid for locked-in, ALS or aphasic patients;
  - detecting or monitoring depression, PTSD, hallucinations, seizures or cognitive decline from decoded imagery;
  - neurofeedback therapy for a named condition;
  - "clinical-grade" accuracy claims;
  - use by clinicians in treatment decisions.
- **Framing that keeps it outside:**
  - labeling as research, art or entertainment;
  - an explicit "not a medical device; not for diagnosis or treatment" statement;
  - no disease vocabulary in UI, documentation or marketing;
  - no clinical thresholds or alerts;
  - use of off-the-shelf sensors in their own cleared or wellness status;
  - software that does not claim to process EEG "for medical purposes."
- **Bundling with a cleared amplifier.** If the software bundles or drives a clinical EEG amplifier cleared under 882.1400, the amplifier keeps its status. The software's own intended use still controls whether it is a device (software as a medical device).
- **Human-subject research.** Research on others (not self-experimentation) that aims at clinical claims could trigger investigational-device rules and IRB review. Noninvasive EEG would ordinarily be a "nonsignificant risk" study. This is general regulatory knowledge, not verified in this session.
- **EU parallel.** The EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR) uses the same intended-purpose logic. Annex XVI of the MDR also brings certain non-medical brain-stimulation equipment into scope, but recording-only EEG is not listed there. This was not verified in this session.

### Gaps
- No FDA statement specific to EEG-to-image or brain-decoding generative systems was found.
- FDA's 2021 guidance on implanted BCI devices for paralysis or amputation was not fetched; it concerns implanted devices only.
- MDR Annex XVI scope was not verified with a source.
- 21 CFR 882.5050 (biofeedback) applicability to consumer neurofeedback was not verified.

## 5. Practical best practices: what the law requires vs what is only recommended

### Takeaway
Few measures are *legally required* of a US developer who never receives data. Once the developer, or a hosted service it runs, processes residents' data, several become required.

| Measure | Required where | Otherwise |
|---|---|---|
| Opt-in consent for neural data | Colorado (if used for identification), Connecticut, Montana; Oklahoma for biometric or health data used for identification; GDPR Art. 9 | Recommended |
| Separate, named-recipient consent for third-party transfer | Montana | Recommended |
| Right to limit use beyond the requested service | California | Recommended |
| Deletion on request and consent revocation | CPA, CCPA, CTDPA, Montana | Recommended |
| Data protection assessments | Colorado, Connecticut, Oklahoma; GDPR Art. 35 DPIA | Recommended |
| Security program | Montana; general "reasonable security" duties | Recommended |
| Notice of LLM training | Connecticut | Recommended |
| Emotion-recognition transparency | EU AI Act Art. 50 | Recommended |

On-device processing, encryption specifics, retention limits, de-identification standards, audit trails and differential privacy are **recommended** (UNESCO, OECD, EDPS/AEPD, Neurorights Foundation, EDPB blockchain guidelines), not mandated as such by any neural-specific statute found.

EEG should be presumed re-identifiable. "De-identified EEG" claims are weak.

### Cited Findings
- **Consent**
  - Colorado, Connecticut and Montana require opt-in or express consent. California requires none, but gives a right to limit. [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2025/07/neural-data-privacy-regulation); [Cooley Montana](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-04-22-montana-on-the-brain-a-bold-step-for-neural-privacy)
  - Montana defines express consent as an affirmative response to a "clear, meaningful, and prominent notice" for a specific purpose. [Vensure](https://vensure.com/employment-law-updates/montana-sets-new-standard-for-brain-data-privacy-with-neurotechnology-law/)
  - Oklahoma has no right to revoke consent. [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20260327-oklahoma-enacts-nations-twentieth-state-comprehensive-privacy-law)
- **Purpose limitation and deletion when the purpose ends** for neural data were proposed in California SB 44, which **failed** (February 2026). They are not law in California. [leginfo](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billStatusClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB44)
- **Assessments:** Colorado and Connecticut require data protection assessments for sensitive-data processing, and Oklahoma does from January 2027. [Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/colorado-amends-privacy-act-with-h-b-1058-adding-new-protections-for-biological-and-neural-data); [Hunton Oklahoma](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/oklahoma-enacts-comprehensive-consumer-privacy-law)
- **Security:** Montana requires a "comprehensive security program." [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-04-22-montana-on-the-brain-a-bold-step-for-neural-privacy). Industry practice is weak: fewer than 20% of consumer neurotech companies mention encryption. [TrustArc/Neurorights](https://trustarc.com/resource/neurotechnology-privacy-safeguarding-the-next-frontier-of-data/)
- **Data minimization and proportionality** are recommended by the EDPS/AEPD and UNESCO. Under the GDPR, minimization is a binding principle (Art. 5(1)(c)) and privacy by design is binding (Art. 25). [MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/240722-it-s-all-in-your-head-not-anymore-eu-data-protection); [Global Policy Watch](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/01/unesco-adopts-first-global-framework-on-neurotechnology-ethics/); [EUR-Lex GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **EEG as a biometric identifier**
  - EEG-based identification reached 98.96% accuracy across 102 subjects in Brigham and Kumar (2010), as reported in an EEG and generative-AI survey. [arXiv 2502.12048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12048)
  - Anonymized EEG patterns can be re-linked to individuals with machine learning, and removing direct identifiers does not prevent re-identification, linkage or inference. Differential privacy offers a stronger formal guarantee. [arXiv 2609.11777 (DP EEG features, 2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.11777)
  - The survey also notes that long-term, real-world identification remains harder than in lab conditions. [arXiv 2502.12048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12048)
  - Regulators (EDPS/AEPD) treat neurodata as identifying "even if the concerned individual is not identified during the collection." [EDPS TechDispatch](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2024-06-03-techdispatch-12024-neurodata_en)
  - Neuroimaging metadata also leaks identity. [PMC 12926773](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12926773/)
- **Audit trails and provenance:** the EDPB says to store salted or keyed hashes or commitments on-chain and originals off-chain. The hash is still personal data. Erasure must remain possible, and "technical impossibility" is no excuse. [EDPB Guidelines 02/2025](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/documents/guideline/guidelines-022025-on-processing-of-personal-data-through-blockchain_en); [Bird & Bird 2026](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/netherlands/edpb-adopts-final-guidelines-on-blockchain-and-personal-data-a-practical-guide-for-organisations)
- **Children:** UNESCO advises against non-therapeutic neurotech for minors. The Senators asked the FTC to apply COPPA to children's neural data. [Global Policy Watch](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/01/unesco-adopts-first-global-framework-on-neurotechnology-ethics/); [Senate Commerce](https://www.commerce.senate.gov/press/dem/release/cantwell-schumer-markey-call-on-ftc-to-protect-consumers-neural-data-2025-4/)
- **High-stakes uses:** DWT recommends that companies prepare for fiduciary-style duties of loyalty and care, and explicitly bar neural outputs from employment, insurance and clinical decisions. [DWT](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/05/all-the-neural-data-and-no-common-rule-2026)

### Inferences
These map the findings onto the system in scope. They are design recommendations, not legal conclusions.

1. **Consent design**
   - Use layered, purpose-specific opt-in, with separate toggles for:
     - (a) recording;
     - (b) storing sessions;
     - (c) training a personal model;
     - (d) each cloud generator, named;
     - (e) research or dataset sharing.
   - Make each toggle revocable, and have revocation delete the dependent artefacts.
   - Together these satisfy the strictest regimes (Montana, Connecticut, GDPR Art. 9(2)(a)) at once.
2. **Minimization and on-device processing**
   - Process on the device by default.
   - Send only the final, user-previewed prompt, never EEG, features or embeddings.
   - Drop physiology channels that the decoder does not need.
3. **Encryption** (already AES-GCM at rest)
   - Keep keys out of the dataset directory.
   - Use crypto-shredding (destroying the key) as the deletion mechanism so it also covers backups.
4. **Retention**
   - Set a default retention period for raw sessions.
   - Keep trained models separately deletable.
   - Document that a personal model trained on EEG is itself derived neural data and may memorize identifying features.
5. **De-identification**
   - Do not label exported EEG as "anonymous."
   - Share only aggregates or differentially private features, and only with consent.
6. **Audit trail / chain**
   - Keyed digests on the public ledger are pseudonymous personal data under the EDPB's view.
   - Keep the key off-chain and per user, so that destroying it makes the digests unlinkable.
   - Never put session metadata (timestamps tied to a person, device serials) on the chain.
   - This matches the project's existing rule that the ledger holds keyed digests only.
7. **Claims hygiene for the FDA** (see question 4): research or entertainment labeling, no disease claims.
8. **Things the law requires only of a business processing others' data**, which a purely local, self-use tool may not trigger:
   - consent records;
   - DSAR and deletion workflows;
   - data protection assessments;
   - a privacy notice including any LLM-training disclosure for Connecticut.
   Build them anyway, because public distribution plus any hosted feature triggers them.

### Gaps
- No statute was found that mandates a specific encryption standard, retention period or de-identification method for neural data.
- No authoritative guidance on whether a personalized model trained on a person's EEG is itself "neural data" under state law.
- The Berlin Group and GPA best-practice texts could not be parsed. Their specific recommendations should be checked before citing them.
- Washington's My Health My Data Act (no threshold, private right of action) may reach EEG-derived "consumer health data". Its text was not verified here, and it may matter for any hosted service available in Washington.
