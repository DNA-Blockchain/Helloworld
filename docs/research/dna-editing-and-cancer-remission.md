# DNA research reaches cancer only through cells

> **Not medical advice.** This is a research summary for software design. Treatment decisions belong with
> an oncologist who knows the patient's diagnosis and history. Nothing in RabbitSoftware treats disease.

As of October 2026, every documented case of DNA-based research bringing cancer into remission has worked through a physical intervention in the body. These interventions are edited immune cells infused into a vein, drugs or vaccines chosen from a sequenced tumour, or fields and heat applied to tissue. None has worked through a digital copy of a person's DNA. A digital twin that holds a genome as binary code, sends it over radio and visualises it with AI cannot change the body or cause remission. No study shows such an effect, the physics rules it out, and every working gene therapy depends on getting molecules into cells. The real advances are worth knowing precisely. Base-edited "universal" CAR7 T cells put about 82% of children and adults with relapsed T-cell leukaemia into deep remission. Allogeneic CRISPR-edited CD19 CAR-T cells produced complete responses in 64–70% of lymphoma patients in small phase 1 cohorts. A personalised mRNA neoantigen vaccine designed from each patient's tumour DNA met its phase 3 endpoint in melanoma in August 2026. Even so, no CRISPR therapy for cancer has been approved anywhere. The digital layer still matters, because sequencing data is what designs these vaccines, tracks residual disease in blood and picks targeted drugs. A local twin can do useful work at that layer: research infrastructure, before-and-after dataset analysis, modelling, organising information for a care team, and supportive care. To do it legally and safely, it has to keep personal genomes off the public chain. *This report is not medical advice. Treatment decisions belong with an oncologist who knows the patient's diagnosis and history.*

## Every CRISPR cancer result so far edits immune cells outside the body

The human evidence follows one pattern. Immune cells are taken out, edited in a laboratory and infused back. No trial has shown benefit from editing the tumour itself. The edits do not correct cancer mutations. They make the immune cells better weapons: an "off-the-shelf" donor product, resistance to the drugs used before infusion, protection against the cells killing each other, or removal of checkpoint brakes. The strongest results are in blood cancers, where the target (CD19 on B cells, CD7 on T cells) sits on cells the infused product can easily reach.

The table shows real progress and also its limits. The allogeneic CD19 products now report complete-response rates that sponsors describe as on par with approved CAR-T. Those comparisons are across trials, not head-to-head. The cohorts have 10–35 patients, and the 12-month durability figure for zugo-cel rests on 3 people. The solid-tumour trials produced mostly stable disease, with isolated long-lasting responses. **No CRISPR-based cancer therapy has been reported as approved by the FDA, EMA or NMPA as of October 2026.** The first approved CRISPR medicine, Casgevy (8 December 2023), treats sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia. It left 29 of 31 evaluated patients free of severe pain crises for at least a year, at a reported list price of $2.2 million ([STAT](https://www.statnews.com/2023/12/08/fda-approves-casgevy-crispr-based-medicine-for-treatment-of-sickle-cell-disease/); [GEN](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/genome-editing/fda-approves-the-first-crispr-therapy-for-sickle-cell-disease/)). All approved CAR-T products are autologous and none is CRISPR-edited ([Cell & Gene Therapy Insights](https://www.insights.bio/cell-and-gene-therapy-insights/journal/article/3912/industry-insights-expanding-access-across-the-field-from-pediatric-approvals-to-the-first-solid-tumor-cart)). The gene-edited allogeneic product closest to filing is Cellectis' lasme-cel. It uses TALEN editing, not CRISPR, received RMAT designation in June 2026 and has a BLA planned for 2028 ([Cellectis 6-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001627281/000117184326004006/exh_991.htm)).

Editing a tumour inside the body has no human efficacy evidence. In vivo CRISPR has worked in people only in the liver. In that trial, NTLA-2001, lipid nanoparticles carrying Cas9 mRNA reduced serum TTR by up to 87% in transthyretin amyloidosis, which is not a cancer. Reviewers say the liver was chosen "because of [its] natural accessibility", and getting particles into dense, poorly vascularised tumours remains "a major hurdle" ([PMC12768121](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12768121/)). Head-and-neck and glioblastoma nanoparticle work is still in mice and cell lines ([Advanced Science 2025](https://advanced.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/advs.202411032); [J Control Release](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0168365924006291)). CRISPR's largest contribution to cancer patients may turn out to be indirect. Genome-wide CRISPR screens found that tumours with microsatellite instability depend on the WRN helicase. That finding led to Novartis's inhibitor HRO761, now in human trial NCT05838768 ([Nature 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07350-y)). The drug is a small molecule, and resistance mutations have already been mapped in the lab ([PMC13434289](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13434289/)).

The limits are concrete. Cas9 cutting causes chromosome loss in up to 20% of edited T cells ([Cell Reports Medicine](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-medicine/fulltext/S2666-3791(24)00617-7); [Nature Biotechnology 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-022-01377-0)). Editing triggers a p53 DNA-damage response that can select against cells with a working p53 tumour suppressor, although how strong that effect is depends on cell type and is disputed ([Haapaniemi 2018](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/CRISPR%E2%80%93Cas9-genome-editing-induces-a-p53-mediated-Haapaniemi-Botla/9e449274b3e69f5d78d924179d96a4941932b19b); [reply, PubMed 31464368](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31464368/)). Base editing, used in BE-CAR7, avoids double-strand cuts for this reason ([Nature Reviews Clinical Oncology 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41571-025-01072-4)). Manufacturing is the most practical constraint. In the CISH trial, only **12 of 22 enrolled patients (55%) were actually infused** ([Lancet Oncology](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanonc/article/PIIS1470-2045(25)00083-X/abstract)). Commercial pressure cut two CRISPR Therapeutics oncology programmes despite "encouraging" data ([CRISPR Therapeutics Q3 2025](https://ir.crisprtx.com/news-releases/news-release-details/crispr-therapeutics-provides-business-update-and-reports-third-6/)).

## DNA tests find relapse months early, but acting on it is the unsolved step

Outside CRISPR, the most mature DNA-based cancer tools read tumour DNA rather than edit it. Tumour-informed circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) testing sequences a patient's tumour, builds a personal assay from that tumour's variants and looks for them in blood. In the GALAXY study (2,240 patients with stage II–III colorectal cancer), patients who were ctDNA-positive after surgery had **36-month disease-free survival of 16.7%, against 83.5% for ctDNA-negative patients**. ctDNA turned positive a **median of 142 days before relapse showed on imaging** ([Natera](https://www.natera.com/company/news/landmark-circulate-japan-study-shows-nateras-signatera-mrd-test-is-predictive-of-chemotherapy-benefit-in-colorectal-cancer-2/); [JCO 2023 abstract](https://ascopubs.org/doi/abs/10.1200/JCO.2023.41.16_suppl.3521); [Nature Medicine 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-024-03254-6)). In blood cancers, clonoSEQ tracks a patient's own clonal immune-receptor sequences down to about one cancer cell in a million ([FDA DEN170080](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN170080.pdf)). These are prognostic tools, and the sensitivity figures come mostly from company-sponsored studies. A single post-surgery blood draw caught 67.6–75% of later relapses, and serial testing raised that to 88–93% ([Natera release](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/natera-announces-updated-signatera-mrd-data-from-circulate-japan-presented-in-plenary-talk-at-the-society-of-surgical-oncology-conference-and-activation-of-circulate-us-trial-301506387.html)). A negative test therefore means lower risk, not cure.

Randomised trials show where detection helps. DYNAMIC (stage II colon cancer, 2022) cut chemotherapy use from 28% to 15% with no loss of 2-year recurrence-free survival (93.5% vs 92.4%) ([NEJM](https://www.nejm.org/doi/pdf/10.1056/nejmoa2200075)). DYNAMIC-III (stage III, 1,002 patients, 2025) separated risk sharply: 3-year RFS was 87% for ctDNA-negative patients and 49% for ctDNA-positive patients. Its de-escalation arm still missed non-inferiority (85.3% vs 88.1%), and escalating chemotherapy for ctDNA-positive patients gave no benefit ([Nature Medicine 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-04030-w)). In ALTAIR (243 patients, 2026), giving trifluridine/tipiracil to ctDNA-positive patients missed its disease-free-survival endpoint ([Nature Medicine 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04428-0)). Across these trials, the blood test is not the bottleneck. The bottleneck is an effective treatment to give once the test turns positive.

Matching drugs to tumour DNA helps a minority of patients, though sometimes a great deal. In NCI-MATCH, 37.6% of tumours had an "actionable" alteration, but only 17.8% of patients were assigned to a matched therapy. Actionability was below 6% in pancreatic and small-cell lung cancer ([JCO 2020](https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.19.03010)). The exceptions show what is possible. BRAF V600E tumours treated with dabrafenib plus trametinib responded in 37.9% of cases across seven tumour types, with a median response duration of 25.1 months. That result helped win a tumour-agnostic approval ([OncLive](https://www.onclive.com/view/dabrafenib-plus-trametinib-elicits-encouraging-responses-in-braf-v600e-mutant-tumors); [PubMed 32758030](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32758030/)).

Personalised neoantigen vaccines are the closest real version of a digital DNA copy driving treatment. The tumour and normal DNA are sequenced, software ranks the mutant peptides most likely to be seen by the immune system, and those peptides are encoded into a bespoke mRNA. Moderna and Merck's intismeran autogene encodes up to 34 neoantigens per patient. In phase 2b melanoma, added to pembrolizumab, it raised 18-month recurrence-free survival from 62.2% to 78.6%. The 5-year hazard ratio for recurrence or death was **0.51**. The 1,137-patient phase 3 INTerpath-001 met its recurrence-free and distant-metastasis-free endpoints on 19 August 2026. Its hazard ratios have not been released, and the vaccine is not yet approved ([Merck](https://www.merck.com/news/merck-and-moderna-announce-phase-3-interpath-001-trial-of-intismeran-autogene-plus-keytruda-met-endpoints-of-recurrence-free-survival-rfs-and-distant-metastasis-free-survival-dmfs-in-patient/); [OncLive](https://www.onclive.com/view/adjuvant-mrna-4157-plus-pembrolizumab-improves-rfs-in-resected-high-risk-melanoma)). BioNTech's autogene cevumeran produced vaccine T cells in 8 of 16 pancreatic cancer patients, and 7 of those 8 were alive about six years later. That comparison is between responders and non-responders, not randomised arms, and randomised phase 2 IMcode003 is recruiting ([Nature 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08508-4); [Medscape 2026](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/pancreatic-cancer-vaccine-still-shows-promise-6-years-out-2026a1000d2b)). The same vaccine, given alone to ctDNA-positive colorectal patients, was stopped in August 2026 for futility ([BioNTech](https://www.biontech.com/int/en/home/mediaroom/news/statements/2026/08/BioNTech-Provides-Update-on-Phase-2-Clinical-Trial-of-Autogene-Cevumeran-in-Resected-Colorectal-Cancer.html)). So far, these vaccines have worked as partners to checkpoint immunotherapy, not as stand-alone treatments.

## Before-and-after data is open only once it is processed

A local twin can analyse real treatment trajectories, but the access tier sets how deep it can go. Raw genomes taken from the same patient before and after treatment are almost always controlled access. A Data Access Committee (DAC) must approve each request, an institution must co-sign, and NIH has required users to follow its security best practices since 25 January 2025 ([NIH](https://grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/sharing-policies/accessing-data/using-genomic-data)). Processed variant tables and expression matrices are often open. They are also small enough to parse with Python on a desktop.

Two gaps stand out. No public dataset was found with serial ctDNA at the variant level. A 2026 benchmark built from MSK-CHORD, GENIE BPC and FLAURA had to rely on single-timepoint features for exactly this reason ([arXiv 2606.11144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11144)). True paired raw genomes are also out of reach for a non-institutional project. The CRISPR data is the cleanest fit for an open, chain-backed tool, because it comes from cell lines and synthetic libraries rather than patients.

Privacy law points the same way as the project's own rules. NIH states that de-identified genomic data can be re-identified ([NIH GDS Policy](https://osp.od.nih.gov/wp-content/uploads/NIH_GDS_Policy_Overview.pdf)). In 2013, researchers inferred surnames from Y-chromosome markers for about 12% of 911 men ([Gymrek et al., Science 2013](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Identifying-Personal-Genomes-by-Surname-Inference-Gymrek-McGuire/c5a4e5024a90aeae16298d8228d67dd9c9182080)). The EDPB's blockchain guidelines (v2.0, 7 July 2026) say even a hash of personal data is personal data. Where something must go on-chain, it should be a salted or keyed digest with the key held off-chain, so that deleting the key can honour erasure ([EDPB v2](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/edpb_guidelines_202502_blockchain_v2_en.pdf)).

## Digital twins change outcomes only through clinicians' decisions

The National Academies define a digital twin as a virtual model, updated with data from its physical counterpart, that predicts and "informs decisions that realize value" ([NASEM 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK605515/)). In oncology, the information flows back to the body only through the drug, dose, schedule or radiation plan a clinician chooses ([NASEM landscape chapter](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK605499/)). This is the right test for any twin. Accurate prediction is necessary, but benefit appears only when twin-guided decisions beat standard care.

Prediction is genuinely good in a few places. MRI-calibrated reaction-diffusion models of triple-negative breast cancer (UT Austin/MD Anderson) predicted change in tumour volume with a concordance of 0.94. They predicted pathological complete response with an AUC of 0.89, against 0.78 for tumour volume alone ([Cancer Research 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9481712/)). A 105-patient follow-up reached an AUC of 0.82 and held up on external I-SPY2 data. Its headline gain of 21–25% in pCR from "optimised" schedules, however, was simulated by treating twins virtually, not by treating patients ([npj Digital Medicine 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11976917/)). The glioma radiotherapy twin was tested on 100 synthetic patients ([arXiv 2308.12429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12429)). Genomics is generally not an input to these clinical-grade mechanistic twins. They run on serial quantitative MRI or PSA.

The only prospective evidence that a model changed outcomes comes from Moffitt's adaptive abiraterone study in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. An evolutionary model set when to pause and restart the drug based on PSA. Median overall survival was **58.5 months for 17 adaptive patients against 31.3 months for 16 standard-of-care patients (HR 0.41)**, and the adaptive patients used less drug ([eLife 2022](https://elifesciences.org/articles/76284); [Nat Commun 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-017-01968-5)). The study was small and not randomised, and its comparison has been disputed in published correspondence ([PMC7804311](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7804311/)). The randomised ANZADAPT trial is still running ([JAMA Oncology 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13448836/)). The European Medicines Agency accepts AI "digital twins" such as Unlearn's PROCOVA, but only as a statistical adjustment that makes randomised trials more efficient, not as a way to choose any patient's treatment ([EMA](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/regulatory-procedural-guideline/qualification-opinion-prognostic-covariate-adjustment-procovatm_en.pdf)). A 2025 review rates oncology twins as "pilot/experimental", and only two oncology-twin studies reported verification, validation and uncertainty quantification ([J Pers Med 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12653454/); [npj DM 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-025-01910-w)). No oncology twin is FDA-cleared to guide therapy.

## A DNA file sent by radio cannot reach a cell

Electromagnetic energy can treat cancer, but only as physical force or heat delivered into tissue, never as a message. Tumour Treating Fields are the proof. Insulated arrays on the skin drive a 1–3 V/cm alternating field at roughly 150–200 kHz through the tumour for many hours a day. The field disrupts tubulin and septins during cell division ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13359516/); [Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep18046)). In randomised trials they extended median survival by a few months, always alongside chemotherapy or immunotherapy:

- **Glioblastoma:** 20.9 vs 16.0 months ([CURE](https://www.curetoday.com/view/tumor-treating-fields-improve-survival-in-glioblastoma)).
- **Lung cancer (LUNAR):** 13.2 vs 9.9 months ([OncLive](https://www.onclive.com/view/fda-approves-optune-lua-for-metastatic-nsclc-after-platinum-based-chemotherapy)).
- **Pancreatic cancer (PANOVA-3):** 16.2 vs 14.2 months. Approved as Optune Pax on 11 February 2026 ([CancerNetwork](https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/fda-approves-ttfields-plus-chemo-in-locally-advanced-pancreatic-cancer)).

In glioblastoma, patients who wore the device longer survived longer, which is what a dose-dependent physical mechanism predicts ([J Neurooncol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11060-018-03057-z)). RF used as heat also works. Regional hyperthermia added to chemotherapy for high-risk soft-tissue sarcoma raised 5-year survival from 51.3% to 62.7% ([JAMA Oncology 2018](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaoncology/fullarticle/2672386)). In both treatments, the benefit scales with deposited energy, frequency and geometry. Information content plays no part.

A radio link from a twin meets none of those conditions, and physics rules out a signal-content effect. The energy of one radio photon is E = hf. At 2.4 GHz that is about 10 µeV, roughly 2,700 times below the thermal energy of molecules at body temperature (about 27 meV) and roughly 360,000 times below a carbon–carbon bond (about 3.6 eV). This is standard physics, and the arithmetic is shown in the research notes. RF acts on tissue by moving ions and rotating water molecules, which makes heat ([PMC7417565](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7417565/)). Cells read DNA with polymerases and transcription factors acting on molecules. Nothing in the body demodulates an FSK packet. ICNIRP limits public exposure to a whole-body average of 0.08 W/kg precisely to prevent heating ([ICNIRP 2020](https://www.icnirp.org/cms/upload/publications/ICNIRPrfgdl2020.pdf)), so any lawful consumer transmitter is far below even the thermal regime. The best-known claim to the contrary is Montagnier's 2011 "DNA waves" and teleportation work. It reported no proper controls, appeared in a journal whose editorial board he chaired, and has never been replicated. PCR contamination is the obvious explanation ([summary with references](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA_teleportation); [arXiv 1104.3113](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1104.3113)). The open scientific question about RF and cancer runs the other way: whether heavy exposure causes harm. IARC lists RF as "possibly carcinogenic" (2B), and a 2024 WHO-commissioned review found moderate-certainty evidence of no brain-cancer link from mobile phones ([IARC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK304630/); [UOW](https://www.uow.edu.au/media/2024/mobile-phones-are-not-linked-to-brain-cancer.php)). The project's own radio module already states the correct position: "the DNA has no radio frequency of its own".

Visualisation and mind-body work help how people feel, but they have not been shown to cause remission. The Simonton guided-imagery method has no controlled trials ([CAM-Cancer](https://cam-cancer.org/simonton-method)). A systematic review found that imagery "may be psycho-supportive and increase comfort" ([DARE NBK71509](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK71509/)). The best-powered trials of group psychotherapy in metastatic breast cancer found no survival effect, though mood and pain improved: Goodwin 2001 (235 women, HR 1.06) and Spiegel's own 2007 replication (31 vs 33 months) ([PubMed 11742045](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11742045/); [Cancer 2007](https://acsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cncr.22890)). A 2026 preregistered meta-analysis of 32 trials found a pooled survival HR of 0.80 for mixed psychosocial interventions. It is fragile: the prediction interval runs from 0.49 to 1.29, mean statistical power was 17%, and no component, imagery included, stood out ([Communications Psychology 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13005039/)). Neurofeedback studies in cancer survivors are small and target fatigue, mood and "chemo brain". Reviewers found "insufficient evidence" even for those outcomes, and none measured tumours ([Integr Cancer Ther 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/1534735415572886); [PMC9893099](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9893099/)). Products that claim tuned frequencies treat cancer are a regulatory problem. No such device is FDA-approved, and one seller of Rife-type devices received 59 months in prison ([Quackwatch, citing court records](https://quackwatch.org/device/reports/rife/folsom/); [WebMD](https://www.webmd.com/cancer/cancer-rife-machine-evidence)).

## What this twin can honestly do, and what it cannot

The project's existing modules map directly onto the parts of this evidence that are real. **Research infrastructure:** the SQLite catalogue, vector store and provenance chain can index the sources above. They can record each dataset release as a fingerprint (release name, SHA-256 of the downloaded file, licence, citation), which matches the "fingerprints only" rule and the EDPB pattern. **Dataset handling and modelling:** `digital_dna.py`, `twin_rna.py` and `swarm_analysis.py` can compare public before/after variant sets and expression matrices against GRCh38. Useful outputs include variants gained or lost, shifts in variant allele frequency, changes in tumour mutational burden, and differential expression between responders and non-responders in GSE91061 or GSE240671. DepMap dependencies can serve as a hypothesis layer: given a profile's mutations, which genes do matched cell lines need? FORECasT and inDelphi data can drive an "edit simulation" that predicts the indel outcomes of a guide on a reference sequence. This is how CRISPR guides are designed in practice, and it stays a computation upstream of any laboratory. The open-source TumorTwin framework ([BMC Med Inform Decis Mak 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12911-026-03520-2)) shows that mechanistic modelling can be done in Python on published, non-identifying data. **Decision support:** a person's own reports can go in the encrypted vault, never on the chain. These include pathology, genomic profiling, ctDNA/MRD results and the list of open trials. The local AI model can organise them into questions for the care team, for example whether genomic profiling, MRD testing or a CRISPR CAR-T or neoantigen-vaccine trial is relevant to that diagnosis. **Supportive care:** `neurovisual/` and the TwinOS sensing work can be developed and honestly evaluated as relaxation or neurofeedback tools, with anxiety, fatigue and quality of life as outcomes. A sham-controlled design would be needed before any claim.

What the twin cannot do is just as clear. It cannot edit, repair or signal to DNA inside a body. Its FSK radio packets carry data between machines and nothing into cells. The AI visualiser cannot shrink a tumour. Neither the twin nor any part of it can cause remission or stand in for an oncologist. Describing it as a treatment would be a medical-device claim that needs FDA clearance and clinical evidence. On data, the twin can safely hold DepMap (CC BY 4.0), BioGRID ORCS (MIT), FORECasT/inDelphi, GDC open masked MAFs and the GEO processed matrices. GENIE (no redistribution) and MSK-CHORD (non-commercial, no derivatives) can be used locally, with only aggregates and release fingerprints published. TRACERx, Hartwig and controlled TCGA/POG raw data are off-limits without an institutional DAC approval. A personal genome, or any per-person hash of it, belongs only in the encrypted vault, with keyed digests on the chain at most.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. The useful question is not whether a digital DNA copy can act on the body, because it cannot. The useful question is where the digital copy already saves lives: tumour sequencing that designs a vaccine, a personal ctDNA assay that flags relapse 142 days early, a screen that finds a drug target. The weak link in 2026 is no longer reading or modelling DNA. It is having an effective treatment to give once the data speaks, as the failed ctDNA-triggered trials show, and getting edited cells to patients before their disease progresses. A local twin earns its place by making that data easier to find, compare and question, and by keeping it private. It must never be presented as a therapy.

For anyone reading this while living with cancer, the hopeful findings are real and specific. Edited immune cells now produce deep remissions in some blood cancers that were once untreatable. A personalised vaccine has cleared phase 3 in melanoma. DNA tests can catch relapse earlier than scans. These are reached through an oncologist and, often, a clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov lists them). It is reasonable to ask a care team whether tumour genomic profiling, residual-disease testing or a trial fits a particular diagnosis. **This report is not medical advice. Please make all treatment decisions with an oncologist.**
