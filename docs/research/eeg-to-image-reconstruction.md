# EEG reads categories; generators paint the rest

As of October 2026, scalp EEG can tell **which kind of thing a person is looking at** well enough to retrieve the matching image from a set of unseen candidates. It cannot recover the picture itself. Visual detail in every published EEG "reconstruction" comes from the diffusion model's prior. The best self-reported within-subject result on the standard THINGS-EEG2 benchmark is **80.5% top-1 in 200-way zero-shot retrieval** (MB2L, an unreplicated May 2026 preprint). The last widely accepted reference point is **50.9% (UBP, 2025)**. Both figures depend on averaging 80 repetitions of each test image. On a **single trial**, top-1 falls to **about 14–20%**, and it drops further on cross-subject models (15–20% even when averaged) and on a 32-channel consumer headset (6% averaged). Imagery and memory recall decode much worse than perception. EEG imagery work that succeeds is **closed-set classification of 3–4 practised items at 60–76%**. Memory reinstatement appears **about 500–1500 ms after a cue, gist first**, and no study reconstructs a specific remembered episode. Even fMRI, which reaches 98.8% retrieval on perception, loses 6–26 points of identification accuracy on imagery. For the `neurovisual` prototype this sets the design: decode a **small personal vocabulary of categories and attributes** in event-locked windows, render them through text prompts, and measure every output against a no-EEG baseline. Legally, a developer in Oklahoma faces no neural-data statute at home: Oklahoma's privacy law takes effect on 1 January 2027 and does not name neural data. Colorado, California, Montana, Connecticut, the EU GDPR and the EU AI Act all apply as soon as any hosted feature receives EEG-derived data, and a decoded text prompt should be treated as neural data.

## Retrieval on THINGS-EEG2 tripled since 2023, but only on averaged trials

The field converged on one benchmark and one recipe. The benchmark is THINGS-EEG2. Ten subjects viewed 16,540 training images from 1,654 concepts, 4 times each. Testing uses 200 images from 200 *unseen* concepts, shown 80 times each, so 200-way top-1 has a chance level of 0.5% ([Song et al., NICE](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.13234)). The recipe has two parts. A compact EEG encoder is trained contrastively to land near the frozen CLIP-style embedding of the viewed image. For reconstruction, that embedding conditions a pretrained generator. The reference pipeline is ATM-S (NeurIPS 2024). It maps EEG through a diffusion prior into CLIP space, builds a blurry low-level image through a VAE path, generates a caption, and feeds all three to **SDXL + IP-Adapter** ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07721)). No dedicated "MindEye for EEG" exists; ATM-S is the de facto analogue and runs EEG, MEG and fMRI through the same pipeline.

The 2025–2026 gains came mostly from the **image side**, not from bigger EEG models. UBP's encoder is **two linear layers (5.4 M parameters)** reading only **17 occipital/parietal channels**. Its key idea is to blur the target image by an uncertainty-weighted amount, to model the foveal and peripheral detail that EEG never carried ([UBP](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04207)). MB2L uses retinotopic adaptive blur ([MB2L](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.04680)) and FSDBN uses SAM foreground masks ([FSDBN](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18344)). MindAlign's best image encoder was a **38 M-parameter CN-CLIP ResNet-50, which beat ViT-L/H/G-14** ([MindAlign](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24523)). EEG foundation models have not changed this. AVDE fine-tunes LaBraM, pretrained on more than 2,000 hours of EEG, and reaches only **30.0% top-1**, about ATM-S level ([AVDE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22555)). Read together, the ablations make one argument: EEG carries coarse, low-spatial-frequency, category-level information, and progress has come from choosing an alignment target that asks for nothing more.

The headline numbers need three corrections before use. First, **trial averaging**: one retriever scores 83.4% top-1 with 80 repetitions averaged, 43.2% with 4 and **14.0% with 1**, and the NEAR denoiser lifts single-trial top-1 only to **19.8%** ([NEAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.19128)). The NEAR authors state that such results "should not be interpreted as real-time or single-trial decoding." Second, **reproducibility**: ATM-S has been reported at 19.7%, 26.1%, 28.6% and 30.2% top-1 depending on version and re-run ([ATM v6](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07721v6); [FSDBN table](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.18344); [Alljoined-1.6M](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18571v1)), and no independent leaderboard re-runs methods under one protocol. Third, **confounds**: an August 2025 audit found that reusing repeated stimuli across training and testing inflated accuracy by **4.46–7.42 points across 16 publications** ([Kilgallen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.00531)).

Reconstruction metrics confirm the generator's role. On THINGS-EEG, published EEG systems cluster at **PixCorr 0.16–0.24, SSIM 0.33–0.40 and CLIP 0.74–0.83**. JMVR leads at PixCorr 0.236 / CLIP 0.829, but it adds a text input, so it is not EEG-only ([JMVR](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19667)). The same ATM pipeline on subject 8 gives PixCorr **0.160 from EEG against 0.305 from fMRI** ([ATM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07721)). MindEye2 on 7T fMRI reaches **98.8% 300-way retrieval, PixCorr 0.322 and CLIP 93.0%** ([MindEye2](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.11207)). Meta's cross-modality scaling study (498 hours, 84 subjects) found 7T fMRI best "across the board". Decoding improved log-linearly with **within-subject** data, while adding subjects brought "modest, if any" gains ([Banville et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15322)). EEG has the cheapest recording time in that study at **$263 per hour**, against $1,093 for 7T fMRI. That cost advantage is the case for EEG; the accuracy figures above are its limit.

## THINGS-EEG2 is the yardstick; block-design results are not comparable

Benchmark choice largely decides what a paper's numbers mean. The early "EEG-to-image" headlines came from the Spampinato EEG-CVPR40 dataset: 6 subjects, 40 ImageNet classes, each class shown as one consecutive block. Li et al. showed in IEEE TPAMI that the high accuracies depended on that block design. Classifiers learned slow block-level brain state, results failed under randomized presentation, and **random codebooks matched EEG-derived representations** ([Li et al., TPAMI](https://doi.org/10.1109/tpami.2020.2973153); [arXiv 1812.07697](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.07697)). The original authors called the critique "drastically overstated". They nonetheless conceded that slow activity inflates accuracy, reported about 50% on new 40-class block data, and reported chance-level results under rapid design ([Palazzo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.03849)). DreamDiffusion (45.8% "50-way" generated-image accuracy), BrainVis, EEGStyleGAN-ADA and Brain2Image all report on this dataset ([DreamDiffusion](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2306.16934); [EEGStyleGAN-ADA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.16532)), so their numbers cannot be compared with THINGS-EEG2 results. EEG-ImageNet v1 also presented same-category images in sequence ([arXiv 2406.07151v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07151v1)).

The protocol is also moving away from averaged trials. Track 01 of the 2026 Neural Interfaces challenge scores a **single EEG epoch** by top-5 retrieval in a frozen DINOv2 space. Its hidden evaluation uses an 11-person Alljoined cohort recorded on consumer hardware ([neural-interfaces26](https://neural-interfaces26.github.io/tracks.html)). That is the protocol a live prototype should report against. MOABB, by contrast, holds only P300, SSVEP and c-VEP datasets and no natural-image data ([MOABB](https://moabb.neurotechx.com/docs/dataset_summary.html)); it is useful only as a loader. One practical hazard: EEG-ImageNet and EEG-CVPR40 ship as PyTorch `.pth` pickles, which can execute code when loaded. The BIDS, EDF and BDF routes through MNE do not carry this risk ([arXiv 2406.07151v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07151v1)).

## Imagination and memory decode as a handful of categories, not pictures

Perception figures do not carry over to imagination. The cleanest evidence comes from fMRI. On NSD-Imagery, human-judged 2-way identification falls from vision to imagery in every method: **MindEye1 84.3% → 73.0%, Brain Diffuser 80.1% → 74.0%, and MindEye2 83.1% → 57.0%**, which is near chance ([NSD-Imagery](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.06898v1)). The best perception model generalized *worst*. Early visual cortex lost the most, and a stimulus's reconstruction quality under vision barely predicted its quality under imagery (r = 0.13–0.22). Two consequences follow for EEG, which starts from far lower SNR. A decoder trained only on perception data (THINGS-EEG2 or Alljoined) should not be expected to transfer to imagery. And an imagery "reconstruction" with a good-looking output is mostly a prior.

Direct EEG imagery results confirm this. The 2025–2026 *Scientific Data* visual-imagery dataset (22 participants, 32 channels at 1000 Hz, 10 cued items) reports EEGNet accuracies of **75.8% for animals, 75.1% for figures and 62.0% for objects**. These are within-category tasks with 3–4 classes, so chance is about 25–33% ([Sci Data](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-025-06512-5)); the exact protocol could not be verified. A 2026 network reports 98.67% within-subject accuracy in 4 ms on CPU ([T&F 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21681163.2026.2684109?af=R)). That task appears to separate the *state* of imagining from perceiving, not the *content* of the imagery, and such near-ceiling figures are typical of state classification. The research for this report found **no EEG paper reporting zero-shot retrieval or standard-metric reconstruction of imagined natural images**.

Memory recall shows a distinct and useful time course. Linde-Domingo et al. found that perception decodes low-level features before conceptual ones, while **associative recall reverses the order: concept first, perceptual detail later** ([Nat Commun 2019](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/files/55251733/Linde_Domingo_et_al_Evidence_that_neural_information_flow_is_reversed_Nature_Communications.pdf)). With repeated retrieval and over time, memories "semanticise", losing perceptual detail relative to gist ([Nat Commun 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-23288-5)). In an 11-person EEG study, encoding patterns for clips seen 3 weeks earlier were still reinstated **more than 500 ms after the cue**; the reinstatement faded with delay and tracked recollection ([JoCN 2020](https://direct.mit.edu/jocn/article/32/1/50/95404/Tracking-Your-Mind-s-Eye-during-Recollection)). Other work places reinstatement **500–1500 ms** after a reminder, alongside the parietal old/new effect ([PMC12256160](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12256160/)). What scalp EEG can expose during recall is therefore a category or semantic code, an old/new or recollection state, and whether the episode is being reinstated. A specific episode is not recoverable. Two cautions apply to all of this. The Shen lab's "Spurious reconstruction" critique shows that pipelines can produce plausible images driven by the prior or the dataset structure ([arXiv 2405.10078](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.10078)). And a 2025 reanalysis questioned the flagship fMRI mental-image reconstruction paper ([arXiv 2511.07960](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.07960)).

## Channels and repetitions, not GPUs, limit real-time use

Compute is no longer the obstacle. NICE trains a subject in **about 5 minutes on one RTX 4090**, and MindAlign in about 8 ([NICE](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.13234); [MindAlign](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24523)). ENIGMA adapts to a new subject with **about 15 minutes of data** ([ENIGMA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10361v1)). For generation, AVDE's autoregressive model needs **91 ms and 1.8 GB per image**, against **310 ms and 4.8 GB** for the ATM diffusion stack ([AVDE](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22555)). Signal physics sets the pace instead. Perception decoding peaks **200–400 ms** after onset and plateaus within 500 ms ([ATM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07721)). Recall needs a window to about 1.5 s after the cue. Imagery trials are self-paced, 3–4 s in the published paradigms. Each decode also carries single-trial accuracy unless the user repeats the stimulus. Meta's MEG work, titled "toward real-time reconstruction", used 100 ms sliding windows and showed a **7× retrieval gain over linear decoders**. It was still offline, used four subjects, and relied on a shielded MEG scanner ([Benchetrit et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.19812)). This research found **no published closed-loop EEG-to-image system with measured end-to-end latency and online accuracy**. A prototype that runs one would therefore be doing something new and should report both numbers.

Hardware quality matters more than any model choice. On identical THINGS images, ENIGMA drops from **27.6% top-1 on research-grade 64-channel EEG to 6.0% on the 32-channel Emotiv Flex 2**, and human 2AFC identification of its reconstructions drops from 83.1% to 65.4%. This is despite 1.6 million training trials and a log-linear data curve that had not saturated ([Alljoined-1.6M](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18571)). Channel gains flatten above about 24 channels, and the informative electrodes are occipital and parietal ([Alljoined-1.6M](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.18571); [ATM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07721)). A low-density 8-channel setup reached **34.4% on 20 image classes** on held-out recordings ([Sci Rep 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-66228-1)). BrainFlow's board list sets the practical choices ([BrainFlow](https://brainflow.readthedocs.io/en/stable/SupportedBoards.html)). Muse 2/S has 4 channels at 256 Hz on the forehead and behind the ears, nowhere near visual cortex, so it suits state signals only. OpenBCI Cyton has 8 channels at 250 Hz. Adding Daisy gives 16 channels but halves the rate to 125 Hz. Ganglion has 4 channels at 200 Hz. g.tec Unicorn, Neurosity, BrainBit and ANT Neuro are also supported. **Emotiv, the only consumer headset with a large image dataset, is not a BrainFlow board.** It has to reach the prototype through LSL or a vendor bridge, and the research did not verify LSL device support. The `neurovisual` sensor layer already has both `BrainFlowEEG` and `LSLEEG` back ends, so this is a configuration choice rather than missing code.

## Oklahoma is silent on neural data, but four states and the EU are not

The developer's home state imposes little. Oklahoma's SB 546 was signed 20 March 2026 and takes effect **1 January 2027**. It applies to controllers handling at least 100,000 consumers, or 25,000 with more than half of revenue from data sales. Its sensitive-data category covers health diagnoses and biometric data *processed to uniquely identify* a person, and **neural data is not named**. Enforcement is by the Attorney General only, at up to $7,500 per violation after a 30-day cure period ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law/20260327-oklahoma-enacts-nations-twentieth-state-comprehensive-privacy-law); [McAfee & Taft](https://www.mcafeetaft.com/signed-sealed-and-soon-to-be-delivered-into-law-oklahomas-new-data-privacy-law-becomes-effective-january-1-2027/)). A public release reaches users elsewhere, though, and four states now regulate neural data explicitly. **Colorado** (August 2024) makes neural data sensitive when it is used for identification ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/colorado-amends-privacy-act-with-h-b-1058-adding-new-protections-for-biological-and-neural-data)). **California** (January 2025) adds it to sensitive personal information and gives consumers a right to limit its use ([leginfo SB 1223](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240SB1223)). **Montana** (October 2025) requires express consent, plus **separate consent that names the recipient for any third-party transfer**; sources disagree on whether its scope extends beyond genetic-data businesses ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2025/2025-04-22-montana-on-the-brain-a-bold-step-for-neural-privacy); [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/)). **Connecticut** (1 July 2026) is the strictest trigger for a small project: it dropped its volume threshold for any controller processing **any amount** of sensitive data, removed its cure period, and requires notices to say whether data trains LLMs ([Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/connecticut-data-privacy-act-2026-amendments/)). Vermont enacted a declaration of neural rights in May 2026 with no enforcement ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-06-23-from-maple-to-mind-taps-new-vermont-law-puts-neurotech-on-notice)). At federal level, the MIND Act would only fund an FTC study and remains in committee ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/2925)).

Most of these duties attach to a *controller*, an entity that processes other people's data. A tool that runs entirely on the user's PC and never sends anything to the developer binds the developer weakly or not at all. That changes with any hosted feature (sync, cloud training, telemetry) or any cloud generator that receives EEG-derived output. The open legal question bears directly on this design: **is a text prompt decoded from EEG "neural data"?** No statute or regulator answers it. California excludes only data "inferred from **nonneural** information", and Connecticut has no inference carve-out, so a decoded prompt plausibly falls inside both. Scholars argue that source-bound rules invite exactly this circumvention and should instead regulate the attribution of mental states to a person ([Horiuchi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00936); [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/the-neural-data-goldilocks-problem-defining-neural-data-in-u-s-state-privacy-laws/)). A prompt such as "a red house by the sea", once attributed to a person's mind, is *more* legible to a third party than raw EEG, because reading it requires no decoder.

The EU adds two points that bear on the provenance chain and on any affect features. The EDPS and AEPD treat neurodata as special-category data that remains identifiable "even if the concerned individual is not identified during the collection" ([EDPS TechDispatch](https://www.edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/techdispatch/2024-06-03-techdispatch-12024-neurodata_en)). EEG identification has reached 98.96% across 102 subjects ([survey, arXiv 2502.12048](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.12048)). The EDPB's final blockchain guidelines (v2.0, July 2026) state that a **salted or keyed hash on-chain "will itself constitute personal data"**. They recommend off-chain originals with commitments that become meaningless once the original and its key are deleted, and they say technical impossibility does not excuse failing to honour erasure ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2025-04/edpb_guidelines_202502_blockchain_en.pdf); [Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/netherlands/edpb-adopts-final-guidelines-on-blockchain-and-personal-data-a-practical-guide-for-organisations)). Under the AI Act, decoding visual content is not emotion recognition. **Inferring affect or intent from EEG is**: that triggers Article 50 transparency, in force since 2 August 2026, and Annex III high-risk duties from **2 December 2027**, after the Digital Omnibus delay. It is banned in workplaces and schools ([Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/eu-ai-act-high-risk-delay-article-50-transparency-consent/); [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/)).

Device regulation is a matter of claims. The FDA's General Wellness guidance (6 January 2026) leaves low-risk wellness software unregulated. It pulls in any product that names a disease, sets diagnostic thresholds or measures physiology "for medical purposes such as screening, diagnosis, monitoring" ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/01/fda-issues-revised-guidance-on-general-wellness-products)). Communication aids for locked-in patients or "detecting hallucinations or depression" would move `neurovisual` toward device status. Research and art framing keeps it outside. Industry practice sets a low bar to beat: in a 2024 audit of 30 consumer neurotech firms, 29 had unrestricted access to neural data and fewer than 20% mentioned encryption ([TrustArc / Neurorights Foundation](https://trustarc.com/resource/neurotechnology-privacy-safeguarding-the-next-frontier-of-data/)). UNESCO's 2025 Recommendation, which is non-binding, advises against non-therapeutic neurotechnology for minors ([Global Policy Watch](https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/01/unesco-adopts-first-global-framework-on-neurotechnology-ethics/)).

## What the evidence implies for each `neurovisual` property

The current prototype maps a rolling 1-second EEG window through a linear map into a 64-value latent. Named slices of that latent ("lighting", "depth", "motion", "camera") become prompt words, and ratings drive a reward-weighted update (`neurovisual/model.py`, `conditioning.py`, `sensors.py`). Against the literature, the architecture is sound in outline. The weakest assumption is **what** it decodes. The evidence supports semantic category and coarse gist. It does not support lighting, framing or camera motion, which are low-level properties that even MEG recovers poorly next to fMRI ([Benchetrit et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.19812)). As built, those slices will be fitted to the user's ratings rather than to the user's brain. The design changes below follow from the findings; where a point is an engineering inference rather than a sourced result, the text says so.

| Property | What the evidence says | Design decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Memory mode | Gist reinstated first, 500–1500 ms after a cue; detail fades over time | Cue-locked epochs at 0.5–1.5 s; decode category plus recollected/not-recollected; the stored description (evidence anchor) supplies the detail, and the UI says so |
| Imagination mode | 3–4-class imagery at 60–76%; perception-trained decoders do not transfer | A closed personal vocabulary of 4–10 practised items, trained on that user's imagery trials; output framed as "steered generation" |
| BrainFlow / LSL | Occipital/parietal channels carry the signal; Muse unsuitable; Emotiv not on BrainFlow | Require an occipital montage (e.g. Cyton at O1/O2/Oz/POz/P3/P4) and refuse or warn for frontal-only boards; Emotiv only via LSL |
| Encrypted sessions | EEG re-identifies; the EDPB expects erasure to remain possible | Keep AES-GCM, a per-user key outside the dataset directory, and crypto-shredding as the delete path for sessions **and** derived personal models |
| Training from ratings | Within-subject labelled data is what scales; ratings are not studied | A short labelled calibration first; ratings refine it as weak labels; every model scored against a no-EEG baseline |
| Text-prompt generators | EEG carries category-level content; text is the readable bottleneck | Prompt = decoded label + user text; preview and edit before any remote send; local ComfyUI/diffusers by default |
| Keyed-digest chain | A keyed hash is still personal data | Per-user HMAC key kept off-chain; no timestamps or device IDs on any shared ledger; never replicate to the public research chain |

**Make windows event-locked, not rolling.** Every positive result above is time-locked to a stimulus or cue: 0–500/600 ms for perception and 500–1500 ms for recall ([NICE](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.13234); [JoCN 2020](https://direct.mit.edu/jocn/article/32/1/50/95404/Tracking-Your-Mind-s-Eye-during-Recollection)). A rolling, exponentially weighted context mixes pre-cue, response and artifact periods. The prototype should emit a marker at each cue, for example on an LSL marker stream, and epoch around it, with a 200 ms pre-stimulus baseline as in THINGS-EEG2 preprocessing. Update rates then follow from the paradigm. Perception and memory modes give about one decode per 1–2 s. Imagery gives about one per 3–4 s. Generation adds seconds: the project's T4 endpoint takes about 5 s warm and 30–60 s cold. The honest label is "online decoding with generation lag", not real-time reconstruction.

**Treat ratings as weak labels on top of a short supervised calibration, not as the training signal itself.** This is an engineering inference; no paper studies rating-based EEG decoder training. The literature gives two anchors. Per-subject encoders train in minutes ([NICE](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.13234)), and new subjects adapt with about 15 minutes of labelled data ([ENIGMA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.10361v1)). Within-subject data scales log-linearly ([Banville et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15322)). A 10–20 minute calibration block therefore gives the model real (EEG, label) pairs: randomized, interleaved presentations of the user's category vocabulary, followed by imagery cues for the same items. Ratings then refine that model. "Yes, that was the dog" confirms a label; a rejection plus a pick from the top-3 candidates corrects one. The risk in rating-only learning is specific. A reward-weighted model can reach high ratings with **zero neural information** by learning the user's aesthetic taste through the generator prior, which is the same failure the "Spurious reconstruction" critique warns about ([arXiv 2405.10078](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.10078)). Each model version should therefore be scored on held-out sessions against two controls: shuffled-EEG and no-EEG (generating from the cue and prior alone). The provenance record should hold that score, not the training loss. Calibration should also follow the block-design lesson. Randomize category order, test on a separate session, and high-pass or detrend to remove slow drift ([Li et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/tpami.2020.2973153)).

**Keep text-prompt conditioning, and use it as the privacy boundary.** Text prompts discard the low-level img2img path that research pipelines use ([ATM](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.07721)). That path contributes little from EEG anyway, given PixCorr of about 0.16–0.24. In exchange, a text prompt is something a person can read, edit and consent to. Following Montana's named-recipient rule and Connecticut's lack of thresholds, every remote send should show the exact prompt, name the provider, and log locally what was sent. EEG, features and embeddings should never leave the PC. Local ComfyUI or diffusers should be the default. Do not map EEG to mood, valence or "emotional lighting". Doing so turns a visual decoder into an emotion-recognition system under the AI Act, with transparency duties and a December 2027 high-risk regime. The existing `contains_memory_text` flag is a good start. A parallel flag marking that a prompt contains an EEG-decoded label would let the consent screen distinguish "your words" from "inferred from your brain".

**Use the provenance chain as a commitment scheme.** The prototype already writes an HMAC-SHA-256 `data_digest` under a key kept on the PC, which is the commitment pattern the EDPB recommends. Three properties need to hold and be documented. The key is per user and stored outside the dataset, so destroying it makes every digest unlinkable. That makes crypto-shredding the erasure mechanism. No person-linked timestamps, device serials or session counts reach any shared ledger. And the `neurovisual` chain stays local rather than replicating to the project's public research ledger, because the EDPB treats keyed digests as personal data for as long as anyone holds the key. Consent should be layered into separately revocable toggles: record, store, train a personal model, each named cloud generator, and research sharing. Revoking one should delete what depends on it. A trained personal model is itself derived neural data, and it can memorize identifying features. Add an 18+ gate, a "research and art software, not a medical device" statement, and no disease vocabulary anywhere in the UI.

Datasets and code fit within these constraints. THINGS-EEG2 (CC-BY 4.0) and Alljoined1 (CC BY 4.0) permit derivatives for population-level pretraining. Alljoined-1.6M and the Gao imagery set are NonCommercial-NoDerivatives, so they suit evaluation only, unless their licence terms are reviewed for redistribution. MIT-licensed encoders are available for NICE, ATM-S, DreamDiffusion, ENIGMA and LaBraM, and UBP is Apache-2.0. AVDE declares no licence, so it cannot be reused. CognitionCapturer's licence differs between its arXiv page and its repository ([UBP repo](https://github.com/HaitaoWuTJU/Uncertainty-aware-Blur-Prior); [ATM repo](https://github.com/dongyangli-del/EEG_Image_decode); [AVDE repo](https://github.com/ddicee/avde); licences checked via the GitHub API on 1 October 2026). Because a UBP-style encoder is two linear layers over 17 posterior channels, a personal model of that shape matches what consumer boards can supply and trains in minutes on the user's own GPU.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what `neurovisual` should claim. The achievable product is not "see what I see" but **"a generator steered by a few things my brain reliably says"**: a category, whether a memory is being recollected, and which of a handful of practised images is being imagined. That claim can be measured honestly, as decoding accuracy above a no-EEG baseline on held-out sessions. No published EEG system has yet reported online, single-trial, closed-loop generation with that measurement, so a prototype that reports it, including when the result is near chance, would add more to the field than another averaged-trial benchmark entry. The most consequential finding for the design is the inversion in recall. Memory arrives concept-first and loses detail with every retrieval. A memory mode built on a stored description plus a decoded "yes, that one" therefore matches the neuroscience better than one that tries to extract imagery from the signal.

On the legal side, neural-data rules key on the *source* while the harm comes from the *inference*, and every live debate points to the decoded prompt as the regulated object of the next few years. Treating that prompt as the most sensitive artefact in the system means previewing it, naming its recipient, keeping it local by default and keeping it off any chain. Doing so satisfies the strictest current regimes (Montana, Connecticut, GDPR Article 9) and anticipates source-neutral rules, at almost no cost to what EEG can actually deliver. Several uncertainties remain open: the 2026 retrieval jumps are unreplicated preprints, LSL device support and Montana's statutory scope were not verified, and whether a decoded prompt is legally "neural data" has not been tested anywhere.
